# Public source material for interpersonal-analysis testing

Checked 2026-09-28. These four directly accessible, first-party pages provide short source passages and surrounding context for behavioral test material. The Bumble profile line is a publisher-written example, not a real user's profile; it avoids exposing a private person's data. Excerpts are under 25 words per source.

## 1. Ordinary dating-profile self-presentation

- **Source:** Bumble, [How to Write the Best Bumble Bio](https://bumble.com/the-buzz/bumble-bio).
- **Exact excerpt:** “If you’re down to rave with me, you have my heart.”
- **Context:** Bumble offers this as a first-person bio example while advising users to emphasize interests and positive phrasing. It is illustrative copy, not evidence about an actual dater.
- **Why suitable:** A brief, appealing self-description leaves most biographical details unstated. It supports testing how the skills handle ordinary selective presentation without treating missing details as evidence of intent.

## 2. Persuasion with differing interests

- **Source:** Bumble, [Everything You Need To Know About Bumble Premium and Premium+](https://bumble.com/the-buzz/bumble-premium).
- **Exact excerpt:** “Using Bumble Premium helps you take the guesswork out of dating.”
- **Context:** The company promotes a paid subscription and describes features such as seeing likes, rematching, and profile visibility. Bumble has an interest in selling the plan; a reader may have an interest in better matches and controlling cost.
- **Why suitable:** The benefit framing and commercial incentive are visible, while the passage alone provides no basis to infer a malicious motive. It offers a commonplace persuasion case within the same dating setting.

## 3. Documented romance-scam behavior

- **Source:** U.S. Federal Trade Commission, [What To Know About Romance Scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams).
- **Exact excerpt:** “Then, they make up a story and ask for money.”
- **Context:** The FTC describes a sequence in which a scammer builds trust through a dating site or social media, may move contact off-platform, and then requests money, sometimes specifying a hard-to-recover payment method. This is consumer guidance about a pattern, not a finding about a particular person.
- **Why suitable:** It supplies observable steps and a concrete requested action for testing analysis of a documented scam tactic.

## 4. Documented coercive behavior

- **Source:** U.S. National Domestic Violence Hotline, [What Is Emotional Abuse](https://www.thehotline.org/resources/what-is-emotional-abuse/).
- **Exact excerpt:** “Your partner makes threats to hurt you or others to get what they want.”
- **Context:** The Hotline lists this among warning signs alongside attempts to control a partner's time, actions, and contact with others. The page is relationship-support guidance, not an assessment of any named individual.
- **Why suitable:** The threat, its target, and the demanded compliance are concrete enough to examine behavior and pressure in context without relying on a personality label.
